Rewinding the Cell

Twenty years after a Kyoto lab found a way to reset a cell’s clock, the idea has reached its first human patients. Now comes the hard part: proving it works.

WordsScience Desk Read4 min Sources15
[ Contents ]A reset button in KyotoFrom mice to peopleThe doubtersThe first answersSources and notes

The treatment arrives the way many eye medicines do, through a fine needle into the eye. What is different is the cargo: three genes that, for the first time in a human being, are meant to make old cells behave as if they were young again.

In June, Boston company Life Biosciences dosed the first patient with the therapy, ER-100, in a small safety trial for glaucoma and NAION, a cause of sudden vision loss.[5][6] It is the first time partial epigenetic reprogramming, one of longevity science’s most hyped ideas, has left the animal lab.

A reset button in Kyoto

The idea began with Shinya Yamanaka. In 2006 his lab at Kyoto University showed that switching on four genes could turn an adult mouse cell back into an embryonic-like stem cell.[1] A year later his team did the same with human skin cells,[2] work that earned him a share of the 2012 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.[3]

The Yamanaka factors work almost too well. Left on in a living animal, they wipe a cell’s identity and can trigger tumours. So researchers tried a gentler version: switch the factors on briefly, or use only some of them, so cells shed the chemical marks of age without forgetting their jobs.[15]

Harvard geneticist David Sinclair became the idea’s loudest champion. In 2020 his lab reported that three of the factors, known as OSK, reversed vision loss in mice with a glaucoma-like condition and in old mice, and helped injured optic nerves regrow.[4]

From mice to people

ER-100 pairs those three genes with a safety switch. They turn on only while a patient takes the antibiotic doxycycline, for about eight weeks, and only in one eye.[6][7] Up to 18 people are due to take part, at Mass Eye and Ear in Boston, Columbia University’s Harkness Eye Institute in New York, a clinic in Glendale, California and the Charleston Neuroscience Institute in South Carolina.[6]

Starting with the eye is deliberate. Before dosing began, Daniel Ives, chief executive of the rejuvenation startup Shift Bioscience, put it plainly to MIT Technology Review: “If it goes wrong, you’ve still got one left.”[8]

Sinclair, a co-founder of Life Biosciences, called the trial “the first opportunity to test whether restoring that information can ameliorate human disease.”[5]

His ambitions reach well beyond eyesight. In January he posted on X that “aging has a relatively simple explanation and is apparently reversible.”[8] He plans to enter an oral reprogramming drug, code-named SL-100, in the $101 million XPrize Healthspan competition.[10][11] “What we’re aiming to do is to epigenetically restore the animal and eventually the person,” he said.[10]

The doubters

Not everyone is cheering. Pete Williams, a neurobiologist at the Centre for Eye Research Australia, fears a failure could set back the whole field: “If this goes catastrophically wrong, it might screw us all in the future.”[9]

The doxycycline switch, built from parts of E. coli and herpes virus genes, has never been tried in humans, so an immune reaction is possible.[8] Sinclair’s animal data on his oral drugs is unpublished, and another Harvard team found that its own chemical cocktail either did nothing in mice or was toxic.[10] Critics also cite his record: in 2024 he stepped down as president of the Academy for Health and Lifespan Research after claiming a supplement had reversed ageing in dogs.[10][12]

The first answers

The first data came sooner than expected. On 8 October, Life Biosciences reported that the first three glaucoma patients had tolerated ER-100 through day 56, that two of the three had improved visual fields, and that the trial’s safety board had recommended moving to a higher dose.[13][14]

Three patients in a safety study cannot show that a treatment works, and the trial was not designed to. Its estimated primary completion date is May 2027.[6] What it can settle is a narrow question: can three of Yamanaka’s genes be switched on safely inside a human eye? A clean result would not prove that people can live longer. But it would mean a reset button found in a Kyoto lab two decades ago had finally been pressed in a person.
