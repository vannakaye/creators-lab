Partial reprogramming, explained

How a tool for making stem cells became longevity science’s biggest bet, what it has shown so far, and what could go wrong.

WordsScience Desk Read2 min Sources10
[ Contents ]Where it comes fromWhat it is meant to resetThe evidence so farThe moneyWhat could go wrongSources and notes

Partial reprogramming is an attempt to make old cells behave like young ones without turning them into something else. It borrows a tool from stem cell biology and uses it gently.[1]

Where it comes from

In 2006 Kazutoshi Takahashi and Shinya Yamanaka showed that four genes, Oct3/4, Sox2, Klf4 and c-Myc, could turn adult mouse fibroblasts into induced pluripotent stem cells: cells that can become almost any tissue.[2] Full reprogramming like this erases what a cell is. Partial reprogramming tries to stop well before that point.[1]

What it is meant to reset

Cells carry a layer of chemical marks on their DNA, the epigenome, which helps decide which genes are read. Those marks change with age in ways regular enough to measure. In 2013 Steve Horvath built an epigenetic clock from DNA methylation at 353 sites, trained on about 8,000 samples from 51 tissues and cell types.[4] Reprogramming winds that kind of clock back. The open question is whether winding it back makes tissue work better.[1]

The evidence so far

In 2016 a team led by Juan Carlos Izpisua Belmonte switched the four factors on in short cycles in mice that age prematurely. The mice showed fewer signs of ageing and lived longer.[3] In 2020 David Sinclair’s lab at Harvard used three of the factors, leaving out c-Myc, to reverse vision loss in a mouse model of glaucoma and in old mice.[5]

In 2026 the idea reached people. Life Biosciences began a first-in-human safety trial of ER-100, which delivers those three genes into one eye of patients with glaucoma or NAION.[10] We covered the trial in Rewinding the Cell.

The money

Altos Labs launched in January 2022 with $3 billion committed, one of the largest raises in biotech, and with Yamanaka as an unpaid senior scientific adviser.[6][7] Retro Biosciences began with $180 million from Sam Altman and works on reprogramming alongside other approaches,[8] and in May 2026 it announced the first close of a new round at a $1.8 billion valuation.[9]

What could go wrong

The same factors that rejuvenate can also erase identity, and cells that forget what they are can form tumours. That is why most groups switch the factors on briefly, use fewer of them, or look for drugs that do part of the job.[1] No partial reprogramming treatment has yet been proven to work in people.
